# AI in K-12 schools: what's working and what's failing

The global K-12 AI rollout of 2023–2026 looks less like a technology revolution than a chaotic natural experiment, with **roughly 60% of US teachers, 64–90% of Estonian students, and two-thirds of American teenagers** already using generative AI tools—often before their schools have any policy. The clearest pattern across dozens of national programs, district pilots, and headline scandals is that **outcomes hinge almost entirely on procurement discipline, teacher training, and student-agency design choices made before any AI tool reaches a child**. Estonia, Singapore, South Australia, France's Pix programme, and a handful of US districts like Newark and Peninsula (WA) have built credible early models. Los Angeles Unified's $6 million "Ed" chatbot, South Korea's ₩1.2 trillion AI Digital Textbook, Lancaster Country Day's deepfake disaster, and the wave of AI-detector false accusations show what happens when schools skip those steps. The stakes are no longer theoretical: by early 2026, deepfake incidents had triggered federal law (the TAKE IT DOWN Act), the EU AI Act had classified school grading and proctoring as "high-risk," and a Pennsylvania court was sentencing 14-year-olds for AI-generated child sexual abuse material made of classmates.

## What separates good adoption from bad

The most consistent finding across RAND, CRPE, EdWeek, Common Sense Media, and OECD data is that **the schools doing this well treat AI as a teacher-and-curriculum problem first, and a technology procurement decision second**. Successful programs share a recognizable shape: a small pilot (8–50 schools), a teacher-built or teacher-augmenting tool rather than a student-replacing one, written policies communicated to parents, and an explicit pedagogical theory—usually some version of "human inquiry, AI produces draft, human reflects." Failed programs almost always invert that order. They begin with a vendor contract or a national mandate, push it to scale without piloting, and discover only afterward that teachers were not trained, parents were not informed, the AI hallucinated, or the tool replicated bias.

The **equity divide is widening faster than adoption itself**. CRPE's 2025 update found the teen income gap in AI use doubled from 12 to 24 percentage points in a single year. RAND's American Educator Panel (Feb 2025) found teachers in higher-poverty schools significantly less likely to use AI or receive guidance. Suburban districts were roughly twice as likely as urban or rural districts to provide AI training. Where schools have explicit AI policies, teacher adoption rises from 60% to 70% and weekly time savings nearly double—but **only 19% of US teachers report their school has an AI policy at all**, and 96% of elementary families either don't know of a school policy or say none has been communicated.

A third pattern: **reflexive bans almost always reverse within months**, and the schools that lose credibility are the ones that ban without offering an alternative. NYC Public Schools (~1M students) banned ChatGPT in January 2023 and Chancellor David Banks reversed in May 2023, calling the original move "knee-jerk fear." Seattle, LAUSD, Baltimore County, Victoria (Australia), and New South Wales all followed similar arcs. The states and ministries that skipped the ban entirely—South Australia, Singapore, Estonia—built more durable programs.

## The good: national AI literacy programs at scale

**Estonia's AI Leap (TI-Hüpe)** is the most ambitious national rollout to date. Announced by President Alar Karis on Independence Day 2025, the program gives all **154 upper-secondary schools** access to ChatGPT Edu and Gemini paid versions, beginning September 2025 with ~20,000 grade 10–11 students and 3,000 teachers, expanding to ~58,000 students and 5,000 teachers by 2027. Funding is **€4 million for year one, split 50/50 between the Ministry of Education and private donors** including Wise co-founder Taavet Hinrikus and Skype/Anthropic investor Jaan Tallinn, with OpenAI and Anthropic as tech partners. The pedagogical core matters more than the funding: a custom Estonian-language learning app, built with the University of Tartu and the Institute of the Estonian Language, **deliberately refuses to give direct answers, scaffolding reasoning instead**. By early 2026, more than 60% of upper-secondary teachers reported using AI weekly. Minister Kristina Kallas framed the bet plainly: "Estonia's true leap is to learn to think with artificial intelligence, not instead of it."

**Finland's Elements of AI**—launched 2018 by the University of Helsinki and Reaktor—remains the global benchmark for AI literacy at population scale. It has now reached **1.8–1.9 million learners across 170+ countries in 26 languages**, is referenced under EU AI Act Article 4 as a model of "sufficient AI literacy," and serves as Finland's primary AI module in upper secondary schools and teacher CPD. The course pre-dates the GenAI hype and is conceptual rather than tool-focused, which is exactly why it has aged well.

**France's Pix programme** quietly built a different model. From the 2026–27 school year, two Pix "AI learning journeys" become **mandatory for over 1.5 million students per year** in Years 9, 11, and first-year CAP, built with INRIA researcher Pierre-Yves Oudeyer. Coverage includes how generative AI works, biases, deepfakes, environmental impact, and prompting. Crucially, Pix is woven into the existing Pix Certification that feeds into French university applications, so it has institutional weight beyond a one-off curriculum.

**Singapore's MOE EdTech Masterplan 2030**, launched September 2023 under Minister Chan Chun Sing, deployed five distinct AI features inside the Student Learning Space—an Adaptive Learning System for mathematics, language feedback assistants for English, short-answer feedback, an Authoring Copilot for teachers, and speech evaluation for mother-tongue languages. Singapore never banned ChatGPT, instead pairing AI tools with explicit ethics modules and the AI4K (AI for Kids) curriculum delivered by 64+ certified instructors across roughly 12% of public primary schools. By 2026 the National Institute of Education's AI@NIE program is offering AI training to teachers at all levels including pre-service, while the Smart Nation Educator Scholarship funds 250 digitally-expert teachers annually.

**MIT RAISE and Day of AI** are the global infrastructure that everyone else builds on. Led by Cynthia Breazeal at the MIT Media Lab, the curriculum is free, Creative Commons licensed, and grade-banded. By 2025 it had reached **more than 2 million students in 175 countries and all 50 US states**, with major deployments including 4,000 teachers and 130,000 students in India, 9,000 teachers and 450,000 students across Latin America, and a November 2025 commitment by DP World and MIT RAISE to make 250,000 teachers and 6 million students AI-literate in 12 months.

**China's mandatory AI curriculum** is the largest top-down program. Beijing's March 2025 directive made AI courses compulsory from fall 2025 with a **minimum 8 class hours per year** at every level, tiered from experiential in primary to innovation projects in senior high. By 2024, 184 "AI education base schools" had been named nationally; Hangzhou, Wenzhou, Shenzhen, and Shanghai built parallel programs; the first national AI textbook from East China Normal University Press, edited by Wang Qingji with SenseTime collaboration, had reached secondary schools since 2018. The state-coordinated approach makes scale possible but raises legitimate concerns—it operates in the same education system that, just a few years earlier, was piloting EEG attention-tracking headbands on primary schoolers.

**India's CBSE AI curriculum** is poised to become the world's largest by raw numbers. A January 2019 elective rollout in Class 9 expanded to a major April 2026 overhaul under Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan: AI/Computational Thinking becomes **compulsory in Classes 9–10 from 2027–28**, with first board exams under the new system in 2029. State-level work matters more in practice—Kerala's KITE trained 80,000 teachers on AI in 2024, Karnataka deployed a "Shiksha Co-pilot" generative assistant, Punjab became the first state to make AI mandatory in government schools. The cautionary backdrop is BYJU's, which collapsed from a $22 billion valuation to founder Byju Raveendran publicly admitting "the company is worth zero" by October 2024, taking down ~80% of Indian edtech funding with it.

## The good: schools and districts integrating thoughtfully

**Newark Public Schools' Khanmigo rollout** produced the strongest measurable learning gain in this entire research base. A three-year longitudinal panel study (Yamkovenko & Grimaldi, 2025) with student and teacher fixed effects across ~8,000 students found that students who mastered 60 additional Khanmigo skills per year averaged **+6 points on NJSLA math—three times the state average gain of +2**. The program scaled from one elementary school in 2023 to 66 schools and 29,000 students. **Hobart, Indiana** (4,000 students under Superintendent Peggy Buffington) became the CBS 60 Minutes case study for districtwide grades 3–12 Khanmigo deployment. The honest caveat: Sal Khan himself acknowledged in late 2025 that "for a lot of students, it was a non-event… they just didn't use it much," and Khan Academy has had to rebuild the product to weave Khanmigo into practice flows rather than treat it as a destination.

**Peninsula School District, Washington** (Gig Harbor) under Executive Director of Digital Learning Kris Hagel became the early-adopter model for thoughtful policy. In July 2023 it released one of the first US district AI policies—drafted via ChatGPT and then teacher-edited—which TeachAI and CoSN now share. In 2025 Peninsula won the AWS Champion Award for "Amplify GenAI" built on Amazon Bedrock and developed in-house tools through what staff call "vibe coding," **saving the district ~$250,000 in edtech costs** versus commercial alternatives. Director Chris Reykdal subsequently made Washington the fifth state with formal AI guidance built on a "human inquiry, AI produces, human reflects" framework.

**Gwinnett County's Seckinger High School** in Buford, Georgia opened in August 2022 as the **first AI-themed public high school in the US**, anchoring a K–12 AI cluster including three feeder elementaries and a middle school. Director of AI Sallie Holloway built a model that **embeds AI literacy across every subject rather than as a standalone class**, with electives consistently over-enrolled. The district's 177,000-student scale matters: this is not a boutique experiment.

**South Australia's EdChat** is the cleanest example of pilot-to-scale done right. Co-built by the SA Department for Education and Microsoft on Azure, it began with an 8-week pilot of 1,500 students and 150 educators across 8 high schools in mid-2023, expanded to 10,000+ students and 2,000 staff, and rolled out to all SA public schools' staff in late 2024 with student access in Term 4 2025. A government evaluation found **94% of student interactions related to curriculum subjects**. **NSWEduChat** followed the same playbook—16 schools in early 2024, then 50, then statewide—with teachers reporting more than one hour weekly saved on lesson resources. Both were built only after the federal Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (December 2023) gave a shared foundation under Minister Jason Clare.

**Oak National Academy's Aila** is the UK's most successful national tool to date. Launched September 2024, built on OpenAI's GPT-4o with retrieval-augmented generation over Oak's national curriculum corpus, funded with £2 million ring-fenced from the £43M Oak budget. Teachers report saving 30 minutes to 4 hours per lesson; ~70% of 2026 trial participants reported saving 4+ hours weekly. The design choice that matters: **Aila intentionally pauses to keep the teacher in the driving seat** rather than handing them finished output.

**Germany's Telli** is a federated GDPR-compliant chatbot built by FWU for all 16 Länder, connecting multiple LLMs (GPT-5, Llama, Mistral) with EU hosting and pseudonymised data. It is **explicitly not approved for grading**—a sensible scope choice given the EU AI Act's August 2026 high-risk obligations. Bremen launched first in June 2025; by mid-2026 roughly half of German states will be on Telli, with the open-source backbone reducing vendor lock-in concerns that plague proprietary alternatives.

## The good: teacher training and curricular infrastructure

The **National Academy for AI Instruction** announced July 8, 2025 by the American Federation of Teachers with Microsoft ($12.5M), OpenAI ($10M over five years), and Anthropic ($500K) commits **$23 million to train 400,000 K-12 educators**—roughly 10% of the US teaching workforce—through a Manhattan facility at UFT headquarters. Critics including the Center for Democracy & Technology's Elizabeth Laird have flagged the obvious risk of vendor capture, but the scale is unprecedented. The Walton Family Foundation/Gallup 2025 survey of 2,232 teachers makes the case for why training matters: schools with an AI policy see teacher adoption rise from 60% to 70%, and the self-reported "AI dividend" rises 26%—though only 19% of teachers report having such a policy and **68% report no school or district training**.

**AI4ALL**, founded by Fei-Fei Li, Olga Russakovsky, and Rick Sommer in 2017, became the template for inclusion-focused programs, expanding from a Stanford summer camp to 16 university partnerships before pivoting in 2022 to College Pathways for historically excluded students. **Code.org's "How AI Works" and "Hour of AI"** target 25 million learners in CS Ed Week 2025 with Microsoft and CSforALL. **Stanford CRAFT**, led by Victor Lee, built classroom-ready resources co-designed with high school teachers across art, math, history, and English. **The TeachAI coalition** (Code.org, ETS, ISTE, Khan Academy, World Economic Forum) published the AI Guidance for Schools Toolkit organized around seven principles—Purpose, Compliance, Knowledge, Balance, Integrity, Agency, Evaluation—that has become the de facto US framework.

The **UNESCO AI Competency Frameworks for Students and Teachers** (September 2024) finally gave international institutions a shared vocabulary: 15 teacher competencies across five dimensions, 12 student competencies across four dimensions. UNESCO notes that as of 2022 **only 15 countries had AI learning objectives in national curricula**; by 2025 it had supported 58 countries in building frameworks. The OECD/European Commission AILit Framework, launched in May 2025 with Code.org support, will underpin the **PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment**—the first international standardized measurement of AI literacy.

## The good (with significant caveats): AI-first schools

**Alpha School** (Austin, Texas) is the most-discussed AI-first model and the one whose claims need the most caution. Founded in 2014 by MacKenzie and Andrew Price, the school replaces classroom teaching for core academics with two hours per day of AI-driven adaptive software (IXL, Khan Academy, Amplify-style tools) and uses afternoons for workshops; there are no teachers, only six-figure "guides." Tuition ranges $10,000–$75,000, with Austin starting around $40,000. The school claims top 1–2% nationally on MAP/AP/SAT and "2x learning," and is expanding aggressively (12 cities by fall 2025, including LA, NYC, and DC). The caveats are substantial: NEPC and independent statisticians have documented "inflated MAP growth ratios" and "misused medians"; tests are entirely multiple-choice with weak writing assessment; Brownsville parents publicly withdrew children citing anxiety from opaque AI-set targets; charter applications have been rejected in California, Pennsylvania, and Utah; and an internal "Alpha Bucks" incentive currency that is central to the model is not publicized. Teachers receiving Alpha transfers report students who compute fast but cannot construct coherent paragraphs.

**David Game College's Sabrewing program** in London opened September 2024 as the UK's first teacherless AI GCSE classroom—initially 7–20 students at £27,000 annual fees, supported by three "learning coaches" not teachers. Co-principal John Dalton called it "a leap of faith," and University College London's Rose Luckin labeled it "an outlier with uncertain future." It has expanded to a 6th-form with 100 scholarships, but there is no measurable outcome data yet.

**Houston ISD's Future 2 schools** under state-appointed Superintendent Mike Miles approved two AI-focused K-8 elementaries in February 2025, with a vision of up to 100 AI-focused campuses, paired with HISD becoming the first US public district to deploy ChatGPT Edu in a "walled garden" in November 2025. The track record is too short to assess.

## The bad: catastrophic procurement failures

**LAUSD's "Ed" chatbot and the AllHere collapse** is the canonical procurement disaster. Los Angeles Unified signed a **$6.2 million, five-year contract with AllHere Education** in mid-2023; ~$3 million was paid before Superintendent Alberto Carvalho and AllHere CEO Joanna Smith-Griffin unveiled "Ed" on stage at the ASU+GSV conference in April 2024. **Within eight weeks the company furloughed nearly all 50 employees, the chatbot was quietly unplugged in June 2024, and AllHere filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy in July**. Whistleblower Chris Whiteley revealed that seven of eight student requests were routed to offshore servers across Japan, Sweden, the UK, France, Switzerland, Australia, and Canada—violating data-minimization rules and exposing sensitive special-education records. In November 2024, Smith-Griffin was indicted in the Southern District of New York for securities fraud, wire fraud, and aggravated identity theft for allegedly defrauding investors of ~$10 million (the indictment alleges AllHere told investors it had $3.7M in 2020 revenue when actual revenue was ~$11,000). By February 2026 the **FBI had raided Carvalho's home**, and the promised independent task force had still not reported. The lessons are mundane and damning: scope was too broad, the vendor too small, the timeline too aggressive, data-privacy compliance unenforced, and the superintendent acted as marketer rather than steward.

**South Korea's AI Digital Textbook (AIDT)** is the largest national-scale failure in this dataset. Launched March 2025 under impeached President Yoon Suk Yeol with a total government commitment of **~₩1.2 trillion (~$850 million)** plus publisher investments of ~₩800 billion, AIDT mandated AI textbooks in elementary grades 3–4, middle school year 1, and high school year 1 for English, math, and computer science. **86% of teachers and parents opposed the rollout**. After Yoon's impeachment, the new National Assembly passed legislation in August 2025 reclassifying AIDT as "supplementary materials"—stripping its legal status as a textbook. Adoption collapsed from 37% in semester one to 19% in semester two, with stark political polarization (Yoon stronghold Daegu hit 98% adoption while Sejong was 8%). Publishers filed administrative lawsuits in April 2025 and a constitutional complaint in December 2025. The failure mode is structural: top-down mandate, no meaningful pilot, no teacher consultation, vulnerable to political turnover.

**Summit Learning**, backed by **$99.1 million from the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative** and built by Facebook engineers, expanded to roughly 380 schools by 2018 before parents and students revolted. The list of rollbacks is long: Cheshire CT (December 2017, after a 7th-grade ancient Rome lesson referenced bestiality); Indiana, PA; Wellesley MA; McPherson and Wellington KS (student walkouts); Brooklyn (students wrote Zuckerberg, "we refuse to allow ourselves to be experimented on"); Boone KY, Fairview Park OH, Kettle Moraine WI. Chalkbeat 2019 reporting found the real dropout rate was 18%, not the 10% CZI/Summit had claimed. Earlier waves of personalized-learning hype crashed similarly: **AltSchool burned ~$174 million** raised at a $440M valuation including investments from Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, and Peter Thiel before winding down lab schools; **Knewton raised $180M+** and sold to Wiley in 2019 for less than $17 million.

## The bad: deepfake crises and inadequate school responses

The deepfake epidemic moved from isolated incidents to systemic crisis between 2023 and 2026. A CDT survey found **40% of US students and 29% of teachers were aware of deepfakes in their schools** during 2023–24, and **6 in 10 teachers were unaware of any school policy** for handling AI-generated sexual images.

**Westfield High School, NJ (October 2023)** was the catalyst case: more than 30 sophomore girls' Instagram photos were transformed into AI-generated nudes by male classmates using the Clothoff app and circulated on Snapchat. Principal Mary Asfendis's school announced victim names over the intercom while quietly pulling boys aside; police investigated but no charges were filed. Victim Francesca Mani (14) and her mother Dorota Mani became national advocates, attending Biden's 2024 State of the Union, joining the TIME 100 AI list, and driving New Jersey's April 2025 deepfake law and—with Texas victim Elliston Berry of **Aledo High School**—the federal **TAKE IT DOWN Act** signed by President Trump on May 19, 2025.

**Lancaster Country Day School, PA** became the most extensive US case. Between October 2023 and May 2024, two 14-year-old boys created **347 AI-generated explicit images and videos of 59 minors plus one adult**, including 48 LCDS students. The school received a Safe2Say tip in November 2023 but Head of School Matt Micciche did not report to police. Only when a parent informed police in May 2024 did the criminal investigation begin. **Lancaster County DA Heather Adams filed 59 felony counts each** against both boys in December 2024; both pleaded guilty in March 2026 and were sentenced to probation plus 60 hours community service. Pennsylvania amended its mandatory-reporting law in December 2024 to define AI-generated CSAM as child sexual abuse material—closing the loophole that had let the school avoid criminal exposure for the delay.

**Beverly Vista Middle School, CA (February 2024)** became the counter-example: Beverly Hills Unified expelled all five 8th-grade boys involved in deepfakes of 16 classmates in a unanimous March 2024 board vote—the first major US case of expulsions. **Issaquah High School, WA (October 2023)** illustrated the opposite failure: school administrators delayed CPS reporting by six days and the prosecutor ultimately declined charges, prompting Washington's HB 1999 in March 2024.

**Spain's Almendralejo incident (September 2023)** affected more than 20 girls aged 11–17 across four middle schools, with a Badajoz youth court convicting 15 minors in July 2024. **South Korea's August 2024 Telegram scandal** was the largest by scale: **500+ schools and universities targeted**, the Korean Ministry of Education identified ~920 school-based victims, prosecutors found one channel alone had over 220,000 subscribers, and 74% of suspects were aged 10–19. Between 2021 and July 2024, only 16 of 793 reported deepfake crimes were prosecuted.

## The bad: AI surveillance and student-monitoring harms

**Gaggle**, monitoring roughly 6 million students across 1,500 districts by 2024, faces an ongoing federal lawsuit filed in September 2024 by nine Lawrence, Kansas students alleging First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendment violations under a $160,000 contract. Student journalists at Lawrence High had documented Gaggle falsely flagging art-student photos as child pornography, missing actual self-harm test messages, and flagging mental-health phrases like "I'm not good enough." A March 2025 Seattle Times/AP investigation found that **2,200 of 22,000 students at Vancouver Public Schools (10%)** were subject to Gaggle alerts in one year, with reporters inadvertently receiving 3,500 unredacted student documents in a records request, including writings about suicide, addiction, and eating disorders.

**GoGuardian**, used in roughly half of US public K-12 schools per company figures, was the subject of EFF's October 2023 "Red Flag Machine" investigation, which obtained "tens of thousands" of alert records and built a public quiz of benign sites flagged as "explicit content," from LGBTQ+ resources to Holocaust education to Black author bios. CDT's 2023 "Off Task" report found 44% of teachers said at least one student at their school had been contacted by law enforcement because of a monitoring flag; **nearly one-third of LGBTQ students said they or someone they know was outed** by digital monitoring. After sustained pressure, Gaggle removed LGBTQ-specific keywords in late 2022 and returned a $25,000 donation to the Trevor Project after the Trevor Project refused it.

**China's BrainCo Focus EDU headbands**, EEG-based attention trackers deployed at primary schools in Zhejiang Province, were suspended in October 2019 after a Weibo backlash reached 77+ million hashtag views and an Economic Daily survey found 88% of respondents opposed the technology. The pattern matters: **even China's relatively controlled environment rolled back classroom surveillance within weeks** under public pressure.

**Pasco County, Florida** ran a predictive-policing program from 2011 that used school data—grades, attendance, abuse history—to build an "At-Risk Target List" of students "destined for a life of crime." Black students were 7% of district enrollment but 23% of expulsions. The Tampa Bay Times exposed it in November 2020; a federal lawsuit, FERPA investigation, and broad coalition action followed, with the program eventually rebranded and phased out by 2022.

## The bad: algorithmic bias in grading, detection, and proctoring

**AI essay-grading bias** is now well-documented. A draft ETS analysis (Johnson & Zhang, June 2024) of 13,121 student essays scored by GPT-4o versus expert human raters found that **GPT-4o penalized Asian American students more than other ethnicities**, with differences not explainable by the data. The Learning Agency's ASAP 2.0 benchmark across ~24,000 essays found ChatGPT replicated the human bias gap between Black and Asian students rather than correcting it, and failed to recognize exceptional writing.

**AI text-detection** is even worse. The 2023 Stanford study by Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu, and Zou (published in *Patterns*) tested seven detectors and found **over 50% of TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers were misclassified as AI-generated**, with near-perfect accuracy on US 8th-grade essays. A simple "enhance word choices" prompt erased the bias. Independent academic studies report 15–26% false-positive rates for human-written academic text. **Marley Stevens**, a University of North Georgia junior, lost her HOPE scholarship and was placed on academic probation after Turnitin and Copyleaks flagged a paper as AI when she had only used Grammarly's browser extension; her TikTok went viral with over 5 million views and Grammarly donated $4,000 to her fundraiser. **William Quarterman** (UC Davis) was cleared after demonstrating that GPTZero flagged Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech as AI. Vanderbilt, multiple universities, and exam boards have disabled Turnitin's AI detector.

**AI proctoring** continues to fail along the same lines. Student researcher Lucy Satheesan's 2021 reverse-engineering showed Proctorio's facial detection failed on Black faces over 57% of the time; a 2022 Frontiers in Education study (Yoder et al.) across four courses confirmed students with darker skin tones and Black students were significantly more likely to be flagged. The August 2022 federal ruling in *Ogletree v. Cleveland State University* held that AI proctoring room scans constitute an unreasonable Fourth Amendment search. The Spring 2021 **Dartmouth Geisel Medical School** debacle saw IT staff retroactively audit 3,000+ Canvas LMS logs, charge 17 students, and pressure them into pleading guilty—charges that were all dropped in June 2021 after EFF and FIRE intervened and pointed out that Canvas's own documentation said logs should NOT be used "in isolation for auditing or other high-stakes analysis."

**The UK Ofqual A-Level algorithm fiasco** of August 2020 remains the canonical algorithmic-bias-in-education case. With exams cancelled by COVID, Ofqual's Direct Centre Performance algorithm downgraded **~40% of teacher predictions**, with state-school and working-class students hit hardest while private-school students were upgraded because the algorithm gave more weight to teacher predictions in small cohorts (which private schools have more of). Within four days the policy collapsed; Sally Collier resigned as Ofqual's chief regulator on August 25, 2020; Boris Johnson later blamed the "mutant algorithm." The International Baccalaureate's parallel July 2020 algorithm controversy was found to violate GDPR Article 22 by the Norwegian government.

## The bad: cognitive offloading and the learning-harm question

Two 2025 studies sharpened the question of whether AI tools are actually helping students learn. **Bastani et al. (PNAS, 2025)** ran a large randomized trial across ~1,000 students in a Turkish high school with three arms: control, GPT Base (standard ChatGPT), and GPT Tutor (Socratic prompting with step-by-step solutions provided). With AI access during practice, performance soared—GPT Base +48% correct, GPT Tutor +127%. **When access was removed for the test, GPT Base students scored 17% lower than the control group**. GPT Tutor neutralized the harm but did not improve over control. ChatGPT's answers were wrong roughly half the time, with step-by-step reasoning errors at 42%.

The MIT Media Lab's **"Your Brain on ChatGPT"** preprint (Kosmyna et al., June 2025) used EEG monitoring on 54 university students writing SAT essays under three conditions: ChatGPT, Google Search, and brain-only. The ChatGPT group showed the **lowest neural connectivity** across the alpha, theta, and delta bands linked to creativity, memory, and semantic processing. Almost no LLM-group participants could quote a sentence from their own essay. A persistence effect emerged: students who used ChatGPT and then had it removed showed less brain activity than students who had been working brain-only all along. The paper is a preprint with a small N and active methodological debate, but its framing—**"the accumulation of cognitive debt"**—has been hard to dismiss.

Common Sense Media's September 2024 survey found **70% of teens have used at least one generative AI tool**, 40% have used it for school assignments, and **46% of those did so without teacher permission**. **83% of parents said schools haven't communicated anything** about generative AI. Pew's late 2025 data showed roughly two-thirds of US teens now use AI chatbots, 30% daily, 54% for schoolwork, and **59% of teens think AI cheating is a regular feature of student life**. The income divide is now stark: higher-income teens disproportionately use ChatGPT and Gemini, while lower-income teens use Character.ai.

## The bigger picture: regulation catches up

The **EU AI Act**, in force since August 2024, will reshape European schools by August 2026. Annex III explicitly classifies as "high-risk" all AI systems used for school admissions, evaluating learning outcomes, assessing appropriate education level, and monitoring or detecting prohibited behavior during tests. Public-sector deployers must complete Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments before deployment, and penalties reach **€35 million or 7% of global turnover** for prohibited practices. Emotion recognition in educational institutions was already banned in February 2025. The implication for EdTech vendors and schools is fundamental: any AI used for grading, proctoring, admissions, or steering learning paths becomes high-risk and triggers conformity assessments, technical documentation requirements, human oversight, post-market monitoring, and incident reporting.

The **US TAKE IT DOWN Act** (May 2025) made nonconsensual intimate imagery—including AI-generated deepfakes—a federal crime with 48-hour takedown requirements. New Jersey, Washington, Pennsylvania, and other states passed companion legislation. **Sweden's partial reversal** to a textbook-first policy under Education Minister Lotta Edholm, with roughly **SEK 1.4–2 billion (~$83–145 million)** committed to physical textbooks through 2026 after PIRLS reading-score declines, is the largest national repudiation of digital-first education and a useful counterweight to AI-maximalist policy.

## What the thoughtful schools do differently

A coherent playbook emerges from the successful cases. **First**, they treat AI as augmenting teachers rather than replacing them—Aila, Telli, NSWEduChat, EdChat, and the Newark Khanmigo deployment all kept teachers in the design loop and in the classroom. **Second**, they pilot at modest scale before mandating, typically 8–50 schools, with measurable evaluation criteria—South Australia's EdChat is the textbook example. **Third**, they write explicit policies and communicate them to parents—the RAND data show that policy alone shifts adoption from 60% to 70% of teachers. **Fourth**, they invest in teacher training before tool deployment, not after; the AFT/OpenAI/Microsoft academy and Singapore's NIE programs are bets that this is the binding constraint. **Fifth**, they design for student agency rather than dependence—Estonia's Estonian-language app refuses to give direct answers, NSWEduChat's student mode uses Socratic questioning, and Bastani's PNAS study confirms that "GPT Tutor" (constrained, scaffolded) avoids the test-score collapse that "GPT Base" (unrestricted) causes.

The failed cases invert each element. LAUSD signed a vendor contract before defining pedagogy. South Korea mandated nationally before piloting. Lancaster Country Day knew about deepfakes for six months without reporting. Pasco County built predictive policing without parent consent. The AI detectors that ruined Marley Stevens's scholarship had never been validated for the populations they were used on.

## Conclusion: a window before the divide hardens

The 2023–2026 K-12 AI rollout has produced enough natural-experiment evidence to call the question. AI tools can deliver measurable learning gains—Newark's three-times-the-state-average math improvement is real—when paired with teacher training, explicit policy, and scaffolded design that preserves student cognitive engagement. They can also waste tens of millions of dollars, expose children's data offshore, falsely accuse students of cheating in ways that destroy scholarships, generate deepfake sexual abuse material of teenagers, and produce measurable test-score declines when withdrawn.

The deepest finding is not about technology but about implementation capacity. The schools and systems with strong existing pedagogical cultures—Estonia, Singapore, Finland, South Australia, Newark, Peninsula—are extracting value from AI because they had infrastructure for thoughtful adoption before the tools arrived. The schools without that infrastructure are buying chatbots that hallucinate, detectors that discriminate, and surveillance systems that out LGBTQ+ students. The 24-point and growing income gap in teen AI use, the 96% of elementary families uninformed about school AI policy, and the 68% of teachers with no AI training together suggest a window of roughly 18–36 months before AI becomes the largest contributor to educational inequality this generation has seen. The lesson from Estonia's AI Leap and South Korea's AI Digital Textbook is the same: **what determines whether AI helps or harms is not the model, but the politics, pedagogy, and procurement that surround it**.
